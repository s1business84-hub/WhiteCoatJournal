A

Description of the Activity

What happened — the factual account

From 13–17 July 2026, I attended the five-day Simulation Acute Medicine Summer School (SAMSS) at Mohammed Bin Rashid University of Medicine and Health Sciences’ Khalaf Ahmad Al Habtoor Medical Simulation Center, in collaboration with IFMSA-UAE. The programme combined hands-on resuscitation, trauma assessment, vascular access and suturing with anaesthesia workshops covering airway management, intubation and spinal anaesthesia. High-fidelity emergency scenarios, crisis resource management, facilitated debriefings and the team-based SimOlympics challenged us to apply these skills in realistic settings.

B

Self-Reflection Using Gibbs’ Reflective Cycle

Gibbs Reflective Cycle

1 Description

Unlike guided skills stations, the simulations presented unfamiliar emergencies without step-by-step expert direction. In one scenario, a man collapsed shortly after his wife had given birth. She was extremely distressed, and our team had to consider the immediate emergency alongside her need for reassurance. We subsequently reviewed our approach during debriefing.

2 Feelings

I was excited to practise procedures in a safe environment, but the unpredictability of the scenario felt more demanding than following instructions in a workshop. The woman’s distress made the situation feel especially real. I became conscious that stress could narrow my attention at precisely the moment a wider awareness of the scene was needed.

3 Evaluation

The anaesthesia and procedural workshops helped me understand individual techniques and their purpose. The simulation exposed a different learning need: recognising priorities when several demands arise together. Working as a team and reflecting afterwards were valuable, although I recognised that completing a scenario is not, by itself, evidence of safe independent clinical competence.

4 Analysis

The contrast between workshops and simulation changed how I understand medical expertise. Knowing how to manage an airway or obtain vascular access matters, but safe acute care begins with recognising threats, using a structured assessment and reassessing as circumstances change. The collapse demanded immediate clinical attention, while the woman’s distress demonstrated why care cannot be reduced to technical tasks. I also appreciated how stress, role clarity and concise communication influence whether a team maintains situational awareness. Debriefing made this learning more meaningful: rather than viewing uncertainty as something to hide, I could see it as a signal to examine my reasoning, seek feedback and practise again. I realised that responsible clinical judgement includes knowing the limits of my training and escalating appropriately.

5 Conclusion

SAMSS showed me that competence in acute medicine requires integrating procedural knowledge with clinical prioritisation, teamwork and compassion. My greatest learning was the difference between performing a rehearsed skill and responding safely to an unpredictable situation.

6 Action Plan

Before future simulations, I will revise structured emergency assessment and anaesthesia-related airway principles. During scenarios, I will state priorities aloud, clarify team roles and reassess after interventions. After each debriefing, I will document one clinical reasoning gap, review the relevant guidance and practise a targeted improvement under supervision.
