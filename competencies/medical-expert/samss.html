A

Description of the Activity

What happened — the factual account

From 13–17 July 2026, I attended the Simulation Acute Medicine Summer School (SAMSS) at Mohammed Bin Rashid University of Medicine and Health Sciences’ Khalaf Ahmad Al Habtoor Medical Simulation Center, in collaboration with IFMSA-UAE. The programme progressed from CPR, BLS, trauma assessment and anaesthesia foundations to practical workshops in IV/IO access, spinal anaesthesia, airway management, intubation, coniotomy and suturing. Human-factors teaching, guided Stop-and-Go simulations, unfamiliar emergency scenarios, structured debriefings and the team-based SimOlympics challenged us to connect procedural knowledge with real-time decision-making.

B

Self-Reflection Using Gibbs’ Reflective Cycle

Gibbs Reflective Cycle

1 Description

One memorable scenario involved a woman who had recently given birth whose husband suddenly collapsed. She became extremely distressed and overwhelmed. Without real-time expert instructions, our team had to assess the collapsed man while responding appropriately to her distress. We reviewed our approach afterwards during a facilitated debriefing.

2 Feelings

The practical workshops were exciting because I could learn techniques in a controlled environment. The unfamiliar scenario felt different: uncertainty, urgency and the woman’s distress added pressure. I became aware of how easily I could focus on the most obvious emergency while overlooking the needs of another person in the room.

3 Evaluation

The skills stations helped me understand individual procedures and their clinical purpose. However, the scenario exposed a limitation: knowing how a technique works does not mean I can immediately determine whether, when or how to use it amid competing priorities. The team setting and debriefing were valuable because they shifted attention from completing tasks to examining our reasoning and coordination.

4 Analysis

The progression from guided workshops to unscripted emergencies reshaped my understanding of medical expertise. Acute care begins with structured assessment, recognition of immediate threats and repeated reassessment, not simply performing the most familiar procedure. The collapsed man required clinical attention, but the distressed mother also needed compassionate communication. I realised that these responsibilities cannot always be addressed by one person at once; clear roles, concise communication and situational awareness help a team respond to both. Human-factors teaching gave me a framework for understanding how stress can narrow attention. Debriefing showed me that uncertainty and mistakes in simulation are opportunities to question assumptions before encountering real patients. Crucially, exposure to intubation or anaesthetic techniques does not establish independent competence; safe practice includes recognising limitations and seeking supervision.

5 Conclusion

SAMSS taught me that effective acute care combines clinical knowledge, prioritisation, teamwork and empathy. My greatest learning was the difference between rehearsing an individual skill and responding thoughtfully to an unpredictable situation.

6 Action Plan

Before future simulations, I will revise structured emergency assessment and airway principles. During scenarios, I will verbalise priorities, clarify team roles and reassess the patient and wider situation. After each debriefing, I will identify one reasoning gap, consult appropriate guidance and practise a specific improvement under supervision.
