Personal Reflection

A

Description of the Activity

What happened — the factual account

From 13 to 17 July 2026, I participated in the Simulation Acute Medicine Summer School (SAMSS) at Mohammed Bin Rashid University’s Khalaf Ahmad Al Habtoor Medical Simulation Center, organised in collaboration with IFMSA-UAE. We began with CPR, BLS and foundations of anaesthesia before practising IV/IO access, spinal anaesthesia, airway management, intubation, cricothyroidotomy and suturing. The programme also included unfamiliar emergency simulations, debriefings and SimOlympics. This activity supports Medical Expert through applied clinical reasoning and patient problem solving.

B

Self-Reflection Using Gibbs’ Reflective Cycle

Gibbs Reflective Cycle

1 Description

One scenario still sticks with me. A woman who had just given birth watched her husband collapse and became extremely distressed. We had to assess him while responding to her distress, without step by step expert guidance. Afterwards, a facilitator helped us review our decisions.

2 Feelings

I enjoyed the skills stations because practising in a controlled environment felt reassuring. Throughout this emergency scenario, I felt uncertain and rushed, and the woman’s distress added to the pressure. What troubled me most was noticing how easily my attention could become fixed on the immediate medical emergency while another person also needed support.

3 Evaluation

The stations helped me understand what individual procedures involved and why they mattered. The scenario exposed a limitation in my preparation; understanding how a procedure works does not automatically mean knowing when to use it amid competing clinical priorities. The debrief was a turning point, shifting my focus from whether we completed the scenario to how we reasoned and worked together.

4 Analysis

Moving from guided workshops to unfamiliar emergencies changed how I understand clinical expertise. Effective acute care begins with structured assessment, recognising immediate threats and continually reassessing, rather than reaching for the procedure I know best. The man required urgent attention, while his wife needed calm, compassionate communication. One person cannot always manage both, making clear roles and awareness of the whole scene essential. Human factors teaching helped me understand how stress can narrow attention. Debriefing showed me that simulation provides a safe opportunity to identify errors and improve decisions without compromising patient safety. Practising intubation in a simulated environment does not establish independent clinical competence. Knowing my limits and seeking supervision are equally important to safe practice.

5 Conclusion

SAMSS showed me that effective acute care depends on clinical knowledge, prioritisation, teamwork and empathy working together. My most important learning was the difference between rehearsing a single skill and responding thoughtfully to an unpredictable situation.

6 Action Plan

During clinical placements, I will observe how teams prioritise emergencies, communicate clearly and support distressed relatives. Under supervision, I will practise structured assessments and handovers, clarify team roles and reassess patients after interventions. I will also apply these lessons to stressful situations outside hospital by listening, staying aware of others’ needs and prioritising calmly. After challenging experiences, I will seek feedback, identify one area for improvement and act on it.
