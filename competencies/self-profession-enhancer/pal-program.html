Peer Assisted Learning (PAL)

Academic Year 2025–2026 · Gulf Medical University

Personal Reflection

A

Description of the Activity

What happened — the factual account

During the 2025–2026 academic year, I served as a tutor and supervisor in Gulf Medical University’s Peer Assisted Learning (PAL) Programme. As a tutor, I supported fellow students with academic material, adapting explanations to approaches that suited them even when these differed from how I studied. As a supervisor, I assigned tutors to students, monitored their required hours, followed up on feedback rubrics and tutor log sheets, and provided guidance. Both roles challenged me to support learning while maintaining accountability.

B

Self-Reflection

Gibbs Reflective Cycle

Step 1Description

Two challenges stood out. In tutoring, I needed to find ways of explaining material that worked for a student whose preferred approach differed from mine. In supervision, I had to repeatedly remind tutors to complete feedback rubrics and submit log sheets on time.

Step 2Feelings

I found tutoring rewarding, although adapting my approach pushed me outside my comfort zone. Repeated reminders could be frustrating because I wanted tutors to take ownership of their responsibilities. I also wanted them to feel comfortable asking for help rather than seeing my follow-ups as criticism.

Step 3Evaluation

A strength was my willingness to adapt and follow through instead of assuming my usual approach would work for everyone. However, these experiences exposed two limitations: an explanation that makes sense to me may not suit another learner, and repeatedly chasing paperwork is less effective than setting expectations and monitoring progress early.

Step 4Analysis

PAL changed how I understand learning and professional responsibility. Adapting explanations taught me to focus on what a student understood, rather than on my preferred way of teaching. It highlighted the importance of checking understanding instead of assuming that agreement means a concept is secure. Supervision revealed a different challenge: hours, feedback rubrics and log sheets are necessary for accountability, but repeated reminders can become reactive. I realised that effective oversight means supporting independence through clear communication, timely follow-up and opportunities to discuss difficulties, not simply checking completion. Together, these roles showed me that empathy and accountability reinforce one another. In medicine, similar skills are needed to explain complex information to patients, support colleagues and uphold professional commitments.

Step 5Conclusion

I learned that effective peer education requires flexibility, patience and reliable follow-through. My greatest area for improvement is to anticipate different learning needs and encourage responsibility before problems arise.

Step 6Action Plan

In future tutoring, I will ask students which approaches help them understand a topic, try alternative explanations and use brief application questions to check learning. As a supervisor, I will clarify deadlines at the outset, arrange regular progress checks and address missing documentation promptly but respectfully. I will seek feedback on my teaching and supervision and identify one improvement after each programme cycle.
